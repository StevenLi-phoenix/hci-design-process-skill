# Ideate: represent the users, then generate options

"It's not about coming up with the 'right' idea, it's about generating the broadest range of possibilities." Stay grounded in the end goal (the problem statement and personas), keep an open mind, and judge feasibility later.

Personas and storyboards sit on the boundary between Define and Ideate. They define the problem's context, and they are the yardstick every idea is measured against.

## User profiles vs personas

A **user profile** lists the characteristics of a whole user group (e.g. ATM customers aged 12–80+, all abilities and education levels, little to expert IT experience). It is useful for coverage, but too broad to design for: one size does not fit all, and age bands invite stereotypes.

A **persona** is "a user archetype you can use to help guide decisions about product features, navigation, interactions, and even visual design" (Kim Goodwin).
- It is a precise description of a hypothetical but representative user who suffers from the problem — a concrete person in the designer's mind.
- It is not a real participant, and not a stereotype.
- It gives the team a shared basis for communication. Reasoning about a specific fictional person with clear goals is easier than reasoning about "users", who have too many conflicting goals.
- **Don't try to design for everybody.**

## Creating personas

1. Identify the major clusters in the analyzed data (affinity headers).
2. Relate them to the problem and goal.
3. **Find the behavior patterns that cut across participants.** Each persona is a *composite*: it combines a pattern seen in several people. Don't produce one persona per interviewee — "Evidence: P2" alone means you described a participant, not an archetype. The number of personas follows the number of distinct patterns, not the number of interviews. With few interviews, merge the overlapping patterns, and say which ones still rest on a single person.
4. Create a hypothetical character who best represents the problem: a name, personality, goals, motivations, frustrations, behaviors. "Like creating the main character of a novel."
5. Set the age to the target user's age, not an interviewee's age.
6. Describe the technologies around the problem: what they use now and the workarounds.
7. Designate **one primary persona**, the main design target, plus **two or more secondary** ("sidekick") personas covering other important patterns.
8. Choose a neutral photo, not a teammate's. Stock or AI-generated images are fine.

**Advice**
- Prioritize attributes and patterns.
- Don't invent idiosyncrasies (a broken arm, a quirky habit) that don't bear on the design.
- Leave out irrelevant detail such as hobbies, unless a hobby is the problem domain.

**Pitfalls**
- Not specific enough: missing goals, motivations or behaviors.
- Stereotypical language.
- **Not validating against the data.** Ask "could this be one of the people we interviewed?" Mark every detail that is an assumption.
- Not reflecting the diversity within the target users.

**Use the persona to scope features.** If the primary persona loves outdoor activities, cruise packages don't belong in the product. Cut features no persona needs.

Industry teams under time pressure sometimes skip Empathize and write personas from opinion. Those are *proto-personas*: label them as assumptions and validate them before relying on them.

## Storyboards (scenarios)

A storyboard is a linear sequence of illustrations that visualizes a story; the *Interaction Design* book calls it a scenario. It is a cheap way to capture, relate and explore experiences.
- **Why it works**: pictures are worth a thousand words; stories are far more memorable than facts; people empathize with characters who face real challenges; meaningful achievement engages them.
- **Its primary purpose is communication**: within the team, to stakeholders, and to funders.
- **It is the story of the user, not the story of the screens.**

**Creating the scenario**
1. Identify the major clusters in the data.
2. Relate them to the problem and goal.
3. Invent a hypothetical but specific and interesting situation that best represents the problem.
4. Include:
   - **Character**: the persona.
   - **Scene**: a real-world context, with a place and a personal story.
   - **Plot**: start with a specific triggering event, and end with the benefit of the solution (or the problem the persona is left with, when the storyboard illustrates the status quo).

**Turning it into frames**
- Break the story into moments: context → trigger → the decisions the character makes → the outcome.
- Show emotions in every frame, using simple facial expressions to mark pain points and relief.
- Use captions, detail and the passage of time where they help.
- Typically 4–8 frames.

**Classic errors**
- **No solution at the end.** The story must resolve through the product.
- **No problem.** A pleasant story with no pain point gives the solution nothing to do.
- **Focusing on drawing quality.** Stick figures are fine; photos with blurred details or simple software work too. A storyboard artist is a communicator, not an illustrator. AI-generated images are acceptable but keeping the character consistent across frames is hard.

A text storyboard is fine when visuals aren't needed: one numbered line per frame with scene, action, emotion and caption.

## Brainstorming

Run at least two rounds. The second comes after feedback on the first and after re-checking the personas.

- **Frame it** with "How might we…" questions derived from the problem statement and the persona's frustrations.
- **Diverge**
  - Quantity over quality; no judging; build on others' ideas; welcome wild ideas.
  - Use timeboxed sketching, e.g. Crazy 8s: eight sketches in eight minutes.
- **Converge**
  - Cluster the ideas.
  - Evaluate them against the persona's goals and the problem's relevance first, feasibility second.
  - Keep 2–3 genuinely different concepts, not one concept and two variants of it.
- **Record why** ideas were dropped, so they aren't revived without new evidence.

## Sketching

- Quick, disposable, many alternatives, annotated with arrows and notes.
- Sketch flows (sequences of screens) as well as single screens.
- The purpose is to think and to compare, not to present.
